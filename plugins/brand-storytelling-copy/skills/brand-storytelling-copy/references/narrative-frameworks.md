# Narrative Frameworks

Story structures, brand voice elevation techniques, and long-form content frameworks.

---

## Core Story Structure

All brand narrative reduces to a version of the same arc. Find the specific version that fits the brand's truth.

### The Founding Story Arc

```
World before (the problem existed and nobody was solving it right)
    ↓
The founder sees something others miss
    ↓
The decision to do something about it (often risky or unlikely)
    ↓
The struggle / the moment everything could have gone wrong
    ↓
The breakthrough / the thing that changed
    ↓
The world now (and the invitation to the reader)
```

The mistake most brands make: skipping the struggle. A story without tension is not a story — it's a press release.

### The Customer Story Arc

```
Where the customer was (the before state — specific and relatable)
    ↓
The moment they recognized something had to change
    ↓
Why they chose this brand (over alternatives)
    ↓
What happened (specific results — not vague improvement)
    ↓
How their life/business is different now
    ↓
The invitation to the reader who recognizes themselves in this story
```

### The Belief Arc (for manifestos and brand POV essays)

```
Here is how most people/the industry thinks about [topic]
    ↓
Here is why that thinking is incomplete/wrong
    ↓
Here is what we believe instead
    ↓
Here is the evidence for our belief
    ↓
Here is what the world looks like if you adopt this belief
    ↓
Here is how we act on this belief every day
```

## The Brand Enemy

The single most clarifying question in brand storytelling:

**What is this brand against?**

Not which competitor company — which way of doing things, which mindset, which status quo? The enemy is a philosophy or approach, not a named competitor. Strong brands stand against something specific.

| Brand | Their Enemy (not a company) |
|-------|---------------------------|
| Patagonia | Disposable consumption; fast fashion mentality |
| Mailchimp | Complexity that keeps small businesses from looking professional |
| Basecamp | Constant distraction and meeting culture in tech |
| Warby Parker | The belief that quality eyewear had to cost a fortune |

The brand enemy gives you:
- A clear villain for the story
- A belief the brand can stand for in contrast
- A community of people who share the brand's values

Write the enemy before writing any brand narrative. It sharpens everything.

## Brand Voice Elevation Techniques

The brand voice in ads is compressed — 125 characters. In brand storytelling, it breathes. These techniques elevate good brand copy to great brand storytelling.

### Specificity as credibility

Every abstraction can be made concrete. Every claim can be made specific.

Weak: "We've been in this industry for a long time."
Strong: "In 2009, I packed the first orders in my parents' garage. Thirteen years later, we ship to 47 countries."

Specificity signals experience. Generic language signals you're hiding behind vagueness.

### The unexpected detail

A surprising, specific, irrelevant-seeming detail makes everything around it feel more true.

Generic: "The morning of launch, our team was ready."
Specific: "The morning of launch, someone brought three dozen donuts from the place with the weird green ones nobody likes. We were ready."

The weird green donuts are irrelevant to the story. They make everything else feel real.

### Sentence rhythm as emphasis

Short sentences hit hard.

Longer sentences build momentum and complexity — they create the sense of an idea unfolding, of a thought that requires more room than a single breath can hold.

Short sentences land.

Use short sentences to punctuate a long build, or to isolate a single idea that deserves to stand alone. Alternate deliberately. The rhythm is not accidental.

### Second person creates intimacy

"You" is more powerful than "our customers." It addresses one specific person — and that person feels seen.

Compare:
- "Our customers often struggle with [problem] before finding us."
- "If you've ever [specific frustrating situation] — this was built for you."

The second version is direct, personal, and immediate. Use it in about pages, manifestos, and any copy that speaks directly to the ideal customer.

### Admitting the hard thing

Brand copy that admits difficulty is more persuasive than copy that claims perfection.

"The first version of this product was not good enough. We shipped it anyway. The feedback was brutal, and it made us better."

Vulnerability builds trust. Perfection is a red flag — readers know nothing is perfect, and when a brand pretends otherwise, the credibility of everything else erodes.

## About Page Framework

### The wrong way to open an about page:

"Founded in [year], [Brand] was created to [vague mission statement]. Since then, we have grown to [number] employees and served [number] customers in [number] countries."

No reader cares about this information. This is the company's perspective on the company. The about page should be the customer's perspective on why this brand matters.

### The right way to open an about page:

Open with the problem, the belief, or the moment. Not the founding date.

Strong openings:
- "In 2012, we spent six months looking for [thing] that met [specific standard]. It didn't exist. So we built it."
- "Most [industry] companies are built to serve investors. We were built to serve [specific customer type] — and those two things rarely go in the same direction."
- "There's a version of [industry/category] that [specific wrong]. We started [Brand] because we believed there had to be a better way."

### About page sections that work:

1. **The founding insight** (not the founding story — the idea that made the brand necessary)
2. **Who we're built for** (describe the ideal customer so specifically they feel recognized)
3. **What we believe** (the operating principles — what the brand will always do, what it will never do)
4. **How we do things differently** (specific, concrete, not vague "we care about quality")
5. **The team** (if relevant — human faces + brief human stories, not job titles)
6. **The invitation** (warm CTA — join us, shop, learn more — appropriate to the brand)

## Manifesto Writing Guide

A manifesto is the brand's declaration of intent. It is not polite. It takes a position.

The key distinction: an about page informs; a manifesto declares.

**Manifesto elements:**
1. **The problem state** — name what's wrong with the world/industry as it currently is
2. **The refusal** — what the brand refuses to accept
3. **The belief** — what the brand stands for instead
4. **The action** — how that belief shows up in every decision
5. **The invitation** — for the reader who believes the same thing

**Manifesto tone notes:**
- Second person or first-person plural ("We" for the brand; "You" for the reader)
- Bold, declarative sentences — no hedging, no "we try to"
- Short sentences for key declarations; longer sentences for context
- Repetition of the central belief is intentional and effective ("We believe. We believe. We believe.")

**Famous manifesto patterns:**

The "Here's what we refuse to do" format:
"We will never [thing the industry does]. Not because we can't. Because we've chosen something better."

The "This is not for everyone" format:
"If you're looking for [comfortable option], we're not for you. We're for the people who believe [specific thing]."

The "Future state" declaration:
"We believe [specific future state] is possible. Not eventually — now. Here's how we're making it happen."

## Thought Leadership Essay Framework

The essay is the most demanding brand narrative format. It requires a specific, defensible argument — not a survey of opinions, not a list of tips.

**The thesis test:** Can you state your essay's argument in one sentence that would make some people disagree? If everyone would agree, it's not a thesis — it's an observation.

Weak thesis: "Content marketing is important for brand growth."
Strong thesis: "Content marketing without a point of view is not marketing — it's noise generation."

### Essay structure:

**Opening (the hook):**
Three options:
1. Start in the middle of a story — in media res
2. Make the provocative claim immediately
3. Pose the question the essay will answer

Never start with: "In today's digital landscape..." / "Content marketing has become increasingly important..." These are the first sentences of essays nobody finishes.

**Stakes (why this matters):**
What is at risk if the reader does not engage with this argument? What is possible if they do? The stakes justify the reader's investment of attention.

**The conventional wisdom:**
Name what most people believe about this topic. This is not a straw man — it's the real thing, stated charitably. Then show why it is incomplete or wrong.

**The counterintuitive truth:**
The argument. State it clearly and defend it with evidence. Use:
- Specific examples (including brand examples and customer stories)
- Data (sourced and specific)
- Logical argument (the reason this is true, not just assertion)
- Narrative (a story that makes the abstract concrete)

**Implications:**
What should the reader do differently based on this argument? The essay is not just intellectual — it should change something the reader believes or does.

**Closing:**
Bring it back to the brand's role in this new understanding. The essay plants a seed; the closing waters it. Not a hard CTA — a natural connection between the argument and the brand's work.

## Tone Register Reference

Brand storytelling spans a range of registers. Match the register to the content type and audience.

| Register | Characteristics | Best For |
|---------|----------------|---------|
| **Intimate and personal** | First person, conversational, vulnerable, specific details | Founder letters, personal essays, welcome emails |
| **Warm and inclusive** | Second person, we-language, inviting, community-building | About pages, manifesto invitations, community content |
| **Authoritative and expert** | Third-person option, data-led, confident, precise | Thought leadership essays, white papers, industry analysis |
| **Bold and declarative** | Short sentences, no hedging, strong verbs, takes positions | Manifestos, brand positioning statements, taglines |
| **Narrative and immersive** | Scene-setting, character, dialogue if appropriate, story beats | Customer stories, origin stories, brand films |

The brand voice does not change across registers — but the register shifts based on context. A warm, approachable brand can write a bold manifesto without becoming a different brand. The voice quality (warmth) is the constant; the register (bold declarations) is the variable.
